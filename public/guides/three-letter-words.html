Three-letter words ranked by how often they fit

Three letters is the minimum answer length in Spliinter. A short word is not worth less than a long one: it earns one point if you are the only player who submits it. The useful question is whether the letters are available in the base word—and whether another player is likely to see the same answer.

What this ranking measures

This table checks every three-letter entry in the current game dictionary against the 91 words in Spliinter’s built-in common-word pool. It counts a word when its letters, including repeats, fit a base word. The percentage is the share of those 91 bases that can form the entry; it is not a prediction of how often it will score in a room.

The pool is the optional BASE_WORDS=common selection, not the much larger default random candidate pool. Results can change if the game dictionary or the hand-picked pool changes. The server’s current dictionary contains 1,301 three-letter entries, but many are uncommon; dictionary acceptance does not mean every player will recognize a word.

Three-letter shapes to scan

After checking repeats, look for simple patterns rather than trying every letter combination.

  • Consonant–vowel–consonant: CAT, DOG, HEN and SUN are examples to test when the tiles fit.
  • Vowel first: ASK, OLD, END and ICE can be easy to overlook if you scan only from the first consonant.
  • Y as a vowel: SKY, TRY and GYM are useful reminders that a short word does not need A, E, I, O or U.
  • Rare letters: JAB, JET, BOX, FOX, SIX, ZAG, ZIP, ZOO, QAT and QUA are in the current dictionary. They only work when the base word supplies every letter.

Rare words can be less likely to collide, but an obscure dictionary entry is not automatically a good guess. Spliinter checks the loaded list, not whether a word is common in everyday speech.

What looks possible but is not

Letter presence and letter count are separate checks. LOCAL uses only letters found in CHOCOLATE, but needs two Ls when there is one. ADD is a real word, but PLAYGROUND has only one D. A two-letter answer is rejected even if it is in the dictionary, and the base word itself is not a valid submission.

For more examples and a practice set, see letter-count traps.

A 60-second pre-round drill

  1. First 10 seconds: count repeated letters and note the vowels.
  2. Next 20: test a few familiar short patterns, including vowel-first and Y words.
  3. Next 20: revisit rare consonants and combine them with available vowels.
  4. Last 10: recount any repeated letter in a candidate before pressing Enter.

Use the drill to warm up your search, not to memorize a fixed list. The round word changes, and a word from this table is playable only when the current letters and dictionary allow it.
