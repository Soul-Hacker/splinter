How to read a Spliinter base word

Before searching for words, scan the tile inventory. Repeated letters change what you can build, while vowels and common stems give you places to start. The numbers below describe Spliinter’s optional common-word pool, not every random round.

Start with the inventory

Look for letters that occur more than once, then count the vowels. A repeated E, S or N can open several word forms; a single copy can make a tempting plural or suffix impossible. The tile row is the resource limit, so note its repeated copies before guessing.

Next, notice combinations that may form familiar stems or endings. A base with S might permit a plural that another base cannot. A base with E and D may suggest a past-tense ending. Those letters are clues, not a promise: the full candidate still needs to fit the inventory and appear in the game dictionary.

What the current common pool shows

For this snapshot, I counted dictionary words of three or more letters that are shorter than each base and fit its letter counts. The calculation used all 91 entries in the built-in BASE_WORDS=common pool and the dictionary loaded by the server on 05 October 2026. The results are not measurements of the default random pool, and the common pool is deliberately small.

The S group has about 2.1 times the average subword count of the no-S group in this particular hand-picked pool. That is an association in a small, selected sample—not proof that adding S causes a fixed score increase. The I+N+G split is a useful counterexample: this group’s average is slightly lower, not higher. Letter combinations matter together, and the dictionary also shapes every count.

The pool ranges from 116 possible subwords for LIGHTWEIGHT to 3,036 for RELATIONSHIP; its median is 531 for EXPLANATION. These totals count dictionary matches, not words a typical player knows or will find in a two-minute round.

Three bases, three search starts

These examples are grouped by their count within the current common pool, not by an objective difficulty rating. The words listed are examples that pass the current validator; they are not exhaustive answers.

  • Lower-count example — LIGHTWEIGHT (116): LIGHT, WEIGHT, WHITE, THIGH, EIGHT, HEIGHT, TIGHT, THE, WET, WIT. Count the repeated Is, Gs, Hs and Ts before exploring endings.
  • Middle example — EXPLANATION (531): PLANE, PLANT, PLAIN, EXPLAIN, EXALT, POINT, LATE, LEAN, ALONE, NATION. Try both the E-X-P-L-A-I-N stem and shorter vowel-rich combinations.
  • Higher-count example — RELATIONSHIP (3,036): RELATION, HEART, PERSONAL, PERSON, LEARN, SHARE, TRAIN, POINT, PLANE, THEIR. Repeated I is available, but most other letters appear once.

The labels describe dictionary opportunity in this one pool. They do not predict how quickly a person will recognize a word, how many other players will submit it, or how many points it will earn.

Use common letters without ignoring the rest

English has many words built around common letters such as E, A, R, T, N, I and O, so those tiles make useful starting points. But a familiar pattern can be shared by many players. Once you have submitted obvious words, inspect less-used letters and combinations that may lead to a less expected answer.

Rare letters are not automatically dead weight. Try a short, dictionary-known candidate if the inventory fits, but do not spend the whole round forcing a letter into an unlikely string. The server’s dictionary—not a general frequency table—decides whether a candidate is accepted.
